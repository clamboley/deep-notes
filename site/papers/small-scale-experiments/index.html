Small-Scale Experiments

Small-Scale Experiments: Are We There Yet?

Lourie, Cho, Ullrich, Lotfi

Scaling laws exist down to 4M parameters, but they only appear on the fully tuned frontier, and small models need hundreds of random-search configurations per scale to reach it. Tuning gets easier as models grow because the hyperparameter loss surface loses effective dimensions. The paper turns this into a methodology (tune thoroughly at small scale, check each assumption with a diagnostic, carry the winner up) and recovers pre-norm's advantage over post-norm from models of 4M to 134M parameters.

Background · §2

Scaling laws, loss as a proxy, and the noisy quadratic limit

The paper combines three results from the literature. Every loss in it is a validation loss in bits per character (BPC).

QWhat is BPC?
Bits per character: the cross-entropy of the validation text in bits (log base 2), divided by the number of characters instead of tokens. It is the average number of bits the model needs to encode each character; lower is better. Dividing by characters makes models with different tokenizers comparable. The paper gets it by changing the log base of the per-character negative log-likelihood that its training library already reports.

Scaling laws and compute-optimal training

A scaling law gives the best attainable loss of a model with p parameters trained on d tokens. Hyperparameters do not appear in it because it describes the fully tuned frontier: each (p, d) is assumed to use its best configuration. Since compute is c = 6pd, the law also says how to split a budget between a larger model and longer training.

Scaling law (eq. 3)
L(p, d) = ε + ζ ⁄ pι + η ⁄ dκ
In words

Loss falls as a power law in parameters and in data toward a floor ε, the irreducible error, which no model reaches.

Compute-optimal allocation (eq. 6)
p* ∝ cκ/(ι+κ),   d* ∝ cι/(ι+κ)
In words

Minimizing the law at fixed compute gives power laws for the best model size and token count. When ι = κ both grow at the same rate: the Chinchilla result, and the reason a tokens-per-parameter ratio is meaningful.

Loss stands in for capability when the data is fixed

Downstream accuracy is hard to predict from scale, since many tasks show emergent, inverse or U-shaped scaling. Mayilvahanan et al. (2025) found a weaker regularity that is enough for research: with the pretraining data held fixed, models that reach the same loss have about the same capabilities, whatever their size or architecture. The data defines a curve of capabilities, the loss picks a point on it, and the scaling law sets the cost of that point. A model family with a better law therefore reaches every capability with less compute, so comparing laws is enough to pick the better model.

The noisy quadratic limit

Lourie et al. (2025a) found that near its optimum, a hyperparameter loss surface is close to a quadratic bowl shaped by a few directions, plus normal noise from the random seed. This has a useful consequence for random search. Each sampled configuration gives one validation loss, so the search produces a distribution of losses, and the lower tail of that distribution must follow a known family of curves. Fitting it needs only the losses, never the Hessian.

Near the optimum (eq. 1)
L(X) ≈ y* + (X − x*)T H (X − x*) + E,   E ~ 𝒩(0, σ)
In words

Around the best configuration x*, the measured loss grows quadratically along the directions that matter, plus seed noise.

Tail of the random-search scores (eq. 2)
P(Y ≤ y) ≈ F(y; α, β, γ, σ)  as y → y*
In words

The share of random configurations that score below y follows a fitted curve. α is the best attainable loss, β how tightly scores pack near it, γ the effective number of hyperparameters (the bowl's dimension) and σ the seed noise.

The approximation only holds near the optimum, in the asymptotic regime: all losses below a threshold θ. If the measured tail follows the fitted curve there, the search has reached the optimum's neighbourhood; if not, it probably missed it. This is the first check of the case study.

§3 · Estimating scaling laws

Tuning is what makes the law appear

The paper fits laws on models of 4M to 34M parameters and judges them by how well they predict held-out models of 67M to 268M. Most choices in the recipe only refine the fit. The amount of hyperparameter search decides whether there is a law to fit at all.

How parameters are counted (with or without embeddings, with or without attention FLOPs) barely matters. Taking the best configuration for each parameter–data budget and decaying the learning rate both sharpen an already visible law, while tying the exponents ι = κ gives mixed results. The search budget is different in kind. With 4 or 16 configurations per scale, the best run found at each scale is still far from the frontier, and the scatter of these suboptimal runs hides the law completely. With 64 it appears but extrapolates poorly; with 256 it is accurate.

A search of hundreds of configurations per scale sounds expensive, but the laws extend down to 4M parameters, where a model trains in under an hour on one GPU.

One sweep, eight token budgets
Each configuration is trained once to 32 tokens per parameter with a warmup-stable-decay schedule. Checkpoints are saved at 1/8, 2/8, …, 8/8 of training, and a learning-rate decay is branched off each one (Hägele et al., 2024). One run therefore gives eight token budgets, each with and without decay, so each scale yields one score distribution per token budget. The two shortest budgets fit the law poorly and are dropped.
§4 · The loss surface across scales

Small models are hard to tune because their surface has more dimensions

As parameters and data grow together, a random configuration becomes more likely to land near the optimum: the distribution of random-search scores piles up into a peak at the best loss. The paper traces this to the geometry of the surface: its effective number of hyperparameters γ falls toward 1.

γ is the intrinsic dimension of the bowl at the optimum, estimated by fitting the noisy quadratic to each sweep. A model can have nine nominal hyperparameters and still behave as if only one or two matter. Fewer effective dimensions make tuning easier because good configurations then fill a much larger share of the space.

Parameters, more than data, drive γ down. Sensitivity itself only collapses when both grow: more data on a tiny model, or a large model trained briefly, barely narrows the gap between the best run and the 25th-percentile run. The authors also tried to model the whole surface across scales and found no simple model that captures its finer details. The consequence for experiments is a division of labour. The small scale is high-dimensional and unforgiving, so it needs extensive search; at large scale, a configuration tuned cheaply transfers with simple rules.

§5.1 · Methodology

Tune small, check every assumption, carry the winner up

The method rests on three findings: loss stands in for capability when the data is fixed, tuned scaling laws exist at tiny scales, and sensitivity to hyperparameters fades with scale. Working at small scale makes it affordable to test each of them instead of assuming it.

A failed check is informative in itself: if the noisy quadratic limit or the scaling law does not emerge, something is likely wrong with the hyperparameters, the model or the implementation. The checks also build a qualitative picture of how the model scales. The paper argues this picture is needed because quantitative extrapolation, even from sound structure, compounds small errors.

§5.2 · Extrapolation I got stuck here

Far from the data, a scaling law reduces to its noisy floor

Small-scale laws extrapolate well across about an order of magnitude. Further out, the prediction is mostly the irreducible error ε, which is the worst-determined parameter of the fit.

In ε + ζ⁄pι + η⁄dκ, the two power-law terms shrink as the model grows, so far enough out the predicted loss is almost entirely ε. But ε is estimated from small models whose loss is still far above it: the fit has to guess where the curve flattens without ever seeing it flatten, and several values of ε fit the same points almost equally well. The paper shows this by fitting independent laws on separate samples of 128 runs per scale, from 4M to 34M parameters: they agree near the data and spread apart far from it, as in the figure below.

Comparing two models by extrapolation therefore ends up comparing their estimates of ε, which are not reliable until the law begins to saturate. The law is most trustworthy near the data, and that is where the paper makes its comparisons.

QDoes the noisy ε make Chinchilla-style compute allocation infeasible?

No. At a fixed budget, ε is a constant added to the loss: it changes how low the optimum is, not where it is. The optimality condition (eq. 5) and the allocation (eq. 6) involve only ζ, η, ι and κ. What ε makes unreliable is the predicted loss far out.

The allocation is exposed to errors in the exponents instead, which compound over orders of magnitude. The Kaplan vs Chinchilla disagreement was of this kind, traced to methodological choices that include scale-dependent tuning (Porian et al., 2024). This answer is reasoning from eqs. 5–6, not a claim of the paper.

§5.3 · Case study

Pre-norm versus post-norm from 4M to 134M parameters

The original Transformer normalized after each residual addition (post-norm). Moving normalization before each sublayer (pre-norm) made training more stable, but the field needed nearly three years and a theoretical analysis (Xiong et al., 2020) to settle that pre-norm is better, an advantage that is largest in deep, expensive models.

The study uses three well-separated scales: 4M and 34M to fit the laws, 134M to validate them. Pre-norm reuses 128 random configurations per scale; post-norm needed more, 511 at 4M, 512 at 34M and 128 at 134M. When parameters and data grow together, compute grows with the square of model size, so a single 1B-parameter run costs as much as 64 runs at 134M or 65,536 at 4M, and the whole study costs about a handful of billion-parameter runs. Each architecture then goes through four diagnostics.

Diagnostic 1 · Is the search tuned? I got stuck here

For each model size and token budget, the paper plots the share of random-search runs whose validation loss is at most y, zoomed into the best runs, and overlays the fitted noisy quadratic and the asymptotic regime where the fit should hold. If the fitted curve stays within the confidence band of the measured one, the search has reached the optimum's neighbourhood, and the best losses can feed a scaling law. The toy below draws the same plot.

Both architectures pass. What differs is the size of the regime. At 4M, the fit holds over the best 7–8% of pre-norm runs but only the best 3–7% of post-norm runs: good post-norm configurations are rarer. This is the study's first finding, that post-norm is more sensitive to its hyperparameters. The toy below shows the same contrast.

QIn the paper's plots, post-norm looks closer to its fitted curve than pre-norm. Did it need more search to look good?
The quality of the fit is not what differs; its extent is. Matching a thin sliver of the best runs is easier than matching a large part of the distribution, and the post-norm plots only show that sliver. Post-norm was searched more, 511–512 configurations instead of 128, and the paper attributes this to its tuning difficulty: when only the top few percent of runs are near the optimum, many more runs are needed to have enough of them to check the curve.

Diagnostic 2 · Will scaling up be easy?

For both architectures, the score distribution moves its mass toward the optimum as models grow, as in §4. Pre-norm grows a clear peak at the optimum; post-norm keeps a second peak at a suboptimal loss, so its tuning stays hard at larger scale.

Diagnostic 3 · Does loss still track capability?

On six multiple-choice tasks, including ARC-Easy, which already shows signal at these sizes, pre-norm and post-norm models of all three sizes fall on one common curve of accuracy against loss. Equal loss gives equal capability, so the architecture with the cheaper law is the better model.

Diagnostic 4 · Do the laws emerge?

With the best configuration per budget and learning-rate decay, both laws predict the held-out 134M models: pre-norm's very well, post-norm's reasonably, probably limited by its tuning. Because far-out extrapolation mostly reflects ε (§5.2), the laws are fitted two ways: each with its own ε (free) or sharing one (joint). Both versions predict the held-out scale comparably, but they disagree beyond it.

Over the tested range pre-norm scales better. With free floors, post-norm overtakes beyond that range, but this comparison rests on the unreliable ε estimates. With a shared floor, which assumes both architectures reach the same final loss on the same data, pre-norm stays ahead and its compute advantage grows. Together with post-norm being harder to tune at every step, the small-scale study recovers the large-scale verdict in favour of pre-norm.

Limitations

Open points

  • Extrapolation needs judgment. Laws exist at small scale but far-out predictions hit statistical limits, so the paper relies on several diagnostics rather than one test.
  • Model-centric research only. Changing the pretraining data breaks the link between loss and capability, so the method does not yet apply to data research.
  • Parametrization. The γ results describe standard training; the authors note they might change under μP or similar parametrizations.
