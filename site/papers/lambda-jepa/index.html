λ-JEPA

Spectral Anti-Collapse Regularization for Self-Supervised Learning

Demirel*, Dominé*, Maiorca, Fumero, Mondelli, Locatello

Joint-embedding methods prevent collapse after the projector, but downstream tasks read the backbone, which can stay low rank. In a two-layer linear network, a negative layer balance keeps every encoder direction alive, and weight decay plus a log-determinant on the encoder selects such a balance. The same penalty, applied to the covariance of view-averaged backbone features, brings a JEPA-style method close to DINO and iBOT, with the best linear-probe transfer at 100 epochs.

Motivation

Anti-collapse in z does not reach the backbone

VICReg, LeJEPA and VISReg apply their anti-collapse term to the projection z = p(h). Linear probes and kNN discard the projector and read the backbone output h. A nonlinear projector can make a low-rank h look full rank, so the term on z is satisfied while h stays collapsed.

On trained ViTs (Fig. 1), RankMe/d, which is 1 when variance is spread evenly over all directions, is far lower at h than at z for LeJEPA and VISReg. Different images also look alike at h: negative-pair cosine is 0.47 to 0.71 there, against about 0 at z.

1Linear theory I got stuck here

Layer balance in a two-layer linear network

The paper borrows a tool from the feature-learning literature. In ŷ = W₂W₁x, the hidden layer h = W₁x plays the backbone and W₂ plays the head. Many factorizations give the same end-to-end map M = W₂W₁, and how the scale is split between the two layers decides whether h can lose directions.

Background: Gram matrices and the imbalance matrix

The Gram matrix W₁W₁⊤ is an Nh × Nh matrix on the hidden space whose entries are dot products between the rows of W₁, the input weights of the hidden units. For a unit direction u of the hidden space, u⊤W₁W₁⊤u = ‖W₁⊤u‖² measures how strongly the encoder writes into u, and the eigenvalues are the squared singular values of W₁. The decoder's Gram matrix W₂⊤W₂ lives on the same space and measures how strongly the decoder reads from each hidden direction.

Layer imbalance (Def. A.1)
Δ = W₂⊤W₂ − W₁W₁⊤,   λ-balanced: Δ = λ I
In words

Decoder read strength minus encoder write strength, direction by direction. λ-balanced means the gap is the same λ in every direction; λ < 0 means the encoder is the larger layer.

Inserting an invertible A between the layers (W₁ → AW₁, W₂ → W₂A⁻¹) leaves the network's function unchanged but changes Δ. The loss is flat along such moves, and under plain gradient flow Δ stays exactly at its initial value (Lemma A.2). The balance is therefore a property chosen at initialization.

Why a negative balance prevents collapse

Non-collapse (Lemma A.4), λ < 0
W₁W₁⊤ = W₂⊤W₂ + |λ| I ⪰ |λ| I,   Ch = W₁ΣxW₁⊤ ⪰ κx|λ| I  if Σx ⪰ κxI
In words

The decoder's strength is never negative, so with λ < 0 the encoder writes at least |λ| into every hidden direction, even one the decoder ignores. If the inputs vary in every direction, so does h: neither complete nor dimensional collapse is possible.

QWith λ < 0, can a few directions be 100× stronger than the rest?

Yes. The balance only sets a floor. A direction that the map uses with singular value s gets a² = (|λ| + √(λ² + 4s²)) / 2 (Theorem A.9), which is about s once s ≫ |λ|, so the spectrum of h can stay very uneven above |λ|. The nonlinear regularizer of §3 adds a trace term that also pulls large eigenvalues down.

2Regularizer

Weight decay plus a log-determinant selects the balance

A special initialization fixes λ only at the start and does not carry over to deep nonlinear networks. The paper instead looks for a penalty whose minimizers, among all factorizations of the same M, are negatively balanced, and which pulls training toward that balance from any starting point.

Linear SACReg (Def. 3.2, Theorem 3.1)
RLSAC = λreg⁄2 (‖W₁‖² + ‖W₂‖²) − γ⁄2 · log det(W₁W₁⊤)
In words

Weight decay on both layers controls the overall scale. The log-determinant, on the encoder only, grows without bound as any encoder singular value approaches 0. Among factorizations of a fixed M, the minimizer has Δ = −(γ/λreg) I, a negative balance.

Dynamics (Theorem A.7)
τ Δ̇ = −2λreg (Δ − Δ⋆),   Δ⋆ = −(γ/λreg) I
In words

Under gradient flow on the task loss plus RLSAC, the task terms cancel in the equation for Δ. Every eigenvalue of Δ decays exponentially to −γ/λreg: the ratio sets the balance, and λreg alone sets the speed.

QWhat does each term do on its own?

Read off the same equation: with γ = 0, weight decay alone drives Δ to 0, the standard balance, which gives no floor. With λreg = 0, τΔ̇ = −2γI, so Δ decreases forever while the encoder keeps growing. Together they meet at −γ/λreg. To converge faster to the same balance, γ and λreg must be scaled together. My reading; the paper does not discuss the separate terms.

3SACReg

The same penalty on the feature covariance

In a nonlinear network Δ is not conserved, and the linear guarantee does not carry over (Remark A.17). The paper keeps the encoder-side placement and moves the penalty from the weights to the covariance of the representation, which any encoder has.

Nonlinear SACReg (eq. 2)
RSAC = λmean⁄2 ‖μ‖² + λcov⁄2 tr(Ch) − γ⁄2 · log det(Ch + εI)
In words

Computed from the mean μ and covariance Ch of a minibatch of features. The trace limits total variance, the log-determinant penalizes small eigenvalues, and the mean term keeps the features from drifting away, which covariance cannot see. For a linear encoder with whitened inputs, tr Ch = ‖W₁‖² and log det Ch = log det W₁W₁⊤, so it reduces exactly to the encoder half of RLSAC (Lemma A.10).

Preferred moments (Theorem 3.4)
μ⋆ = 0,   Ch⋆ = (γ/λcov − ε)+ I
In words

On its own, the regularizer prefers centered features with the same variance in every direction. With ε = 0 it diverges as soon as any eigenvalue reaches 0 (Theorem A.15).

QWhy not just add SIGReg at the backbone too?

The paper tried it (Appendix D): LeJEPA on ImageNet-100 with a second SIGReg on the 512-d encoder output. The Gaussianity statistics at h improve a lot (negative-pair cosine 0.58 → 0.01, diagonal KL 0.96 → 0.07), but the spectrum stays concentrated (RankMe/d 0.07 → 0.09) and accuracy drops (linear probe 60.4 → 58.9, kNN 52.4 → 48.1). SIGReg tests one-dimensional projections against a Gaussian; the log-determinant acts on the covariance spectrum directly.

4Method

λ-JEPA: SACReg on view centers, at the backbone and the projection

Each image i gives V augmented views, encoded to h and projected to z. SACReg is applied to the average of the views, the view center h̄i, so it spreads images apart without asking the views of one image to differ. The invariance loss pulls the views' z together.

SACReg for JE-SSL (Def. 3.5)
SACReg(R) = ½ (‖μR‖² + tr ΣR − log det(ΣR + εI) − d)
Objective (eq. 3)
Lλ-JEPA = LSSL(Z) + βh SACReg(H̄) + βz SACReg(Z̄),   LSSL ∝ Σi Σj<k ‖zi(j) − zi(k)‖²
In words

All weights inside SACReg are 1, so the preferred covariance is the identity and the −d makes the minimum 0. H̄ and Z̄ are the batch's view centers. The SSL loss only asks views of one image to agree; it has no negatives and no anti-collapse term of its own (App. B). When the backbone term is added to another method, that method's own loss is kept and only βh SACReg(H̄) is added.

Making the log-determinant usable. B view centers give a covariance of rank at most B − 1, so when the feature dimension d reaches the batch size the log-determinant is degenerate. Each step, the centers are projected onto a fresh random orthonormal slice of d′ dimensions (a third of the width at the backbone, 128 after the projector) and SACReg is computed there. Averaged over slices, the objective keeps the same unique minimizer and approaches the full one as d′ grows (Prop. B.1). A ring buffer of detached centers from previous steps keeps four centers per slice dimension, and only the current batch receives gradients.

Loss weights

Set by gradient share rather than by tuning: each weight is chosen so that its term gets a target fraction of the total backbone-gradient norm, measured on a pilot run. For λ-JEPA the fractions are 0.44 for invariance, 0.54 for the projection term and 0.02 for the backbone term; when the backbone term is added to another method it gets 0.06. Only DINO's weight was tuned by hand.

QDoes it work with gradient accumulation?

Not naively, as with SIGReg: the log-determinant couples the whole batch, and a micro-batch of 8 centers cannot estimate a 128-d covariance. The ring buffer is part of the answer, since during accumulation the earlier micro-batches' centers come from the same weights. For exact gradients, the GradCache trick (Gao et al., 2021) computes all centers without gradients, takes ∂L/∂center on the full set, then re-runs each micro-batch and backpropagates those cached gradients, for one extra forward pass. Each image's views stay in one micro-batch, and the slice is shared across micro-batches. My reasoning, not in the paper.

QDoes it cost more than LeJEPA?

The paper reports no throughput. The regularizer itself (a QR, a covariance of at most 256 × 256 and a log-determinant per step) is negligible next to the ViT, so cost differences come from the view recipe, which is independent of the loss. Comparisons in the paper are matched in epochs, not compute. My estimate.

Analysis

What the extra directions contain

On ImageNet-100, the backbone term was added to six methods (LeJEPA, VICReg, VISReg, SimCLR, DINO, BYOL) with their losses unchanged. Backbone RankMe rose for every method, negative-pair cosine moved toward 0, and kNN accuracy improved for all six, while the statistics of the projection barely changed (Fig. 2, 7).

Positive pairs also became less similar, so the gains do not come from more invariance. To see where they come from, the paper splits the backbone covariance into variation between images and variation across views of one image.

Augmentation thickness (eq. 41, 42)
Ch = Bh + Ah,  Bh = Cov(𝔼[h | Q]),  Ah = 𝔼[Cov(h | Q)],  Θh = Bh†/2 Ah Bh†/2
In words

Q is a source image. Bh is the spread of image centers, Ah the spread of views around their center, and Θh the second measured in units of the first.

Error from one view (eq. 49)
minu 𝔼(y − u⊤h)² = dh(y)² + a⊤Θh(I + Θh)⁻¹a
In words

Predicting a class indicator y linearly from a single view costs two terms: how badly the image centers predict it (class-center separability is 1 − dh²) and a view-sensitivity term that grows with thickness along the class direction a. Too little thickness can discard useful variation (eq. 47); too much drowns the image centers.

With SACReg, all six methods gain class-center separability, and view sensitivity rises as well. The regularizer improves how image centers are organized along class directions rather than removing augmentation variation. For λ-JEPA, separability improves for 96 of 100 classes.

Results

Close to DINO and iBOT, best transfer at 100 epochs

Longer training. At 400 epochs with the same hyperparameters, ViT-B reaches 76.0 linear and 82.0 transfer: 2.9 transfer points above 400-epoch VISReg and within 1.2 of DINO and iBOT (Tab. 1b).

Video. Swapped into the LeVJEPA pipeline, the ViT-B reaches 43.9 on Something-Something-v2 at 240 epochs against 30.4 for LeVJEPA, and 48.3 against 40.4 at 1,085 epochs (Tab. 2). These use a probe on the CLS token, the representation SACReg regularizes; mean-pooled features, which it does not touch, do worse (43.4 against 45.7 on Kinetics-400).

Limitations

Open points

  • Global views and CLS only. Local crops made the view centers noisy and training unstable, so patch features and dense prediction remain untested (Sec. 5).
  • No guarantee beyond the linear case. Δ is not conserved in nonlinear networks; there, the covariance penalty is motivated by the linear analysis and its anti-collapse property is proven for the regularizer alone (Remark A.17).
  • Comparisons not fully matched. Long runs reuse the 100-epoch hyperparameters, baselines differ in epochs, and the OK-AI baseline checkpoints were trained on 1.45M images (App. C.5).
